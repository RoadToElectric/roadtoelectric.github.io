How these numbers are built — read before quoting them
  1. Source: United Nations, Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division — World Population Prospects 2024, medium variant, population by single year of age (both sexes, 1 July), 237 countries and areas plus the UN's own regions. 1950–2023 are UN estimates; 2024 onward (hatched on the charts) are projections — so today's figures for 2024–2026 are projected too. Revised by the UN every two years.
  2. Age groups: under 16 = ages 0–15, 16–64, 65+ (these three never overlap and add up to the total). “Adults” / potential drivers = everyone from the start-driving age (16 to 21, default 18) up to the stop-driving age (60 to 99, default 80) — driving ages differ by country and by person, so both thresholds are settings, not facts. The hero and the “adults” figures elsewhere count everyone 18 and over, with no upper limit.
  3. Potential drivers are an upper bound, not licensed drivers. The number counts people who are old enough; how many actually hold a licence or own a car depends on income, cities and culture. Use it to size the pool of people entering the driving market, not the number of cars.
  4. Net gain vs people reaching the age: net gain is the change in the number of people aged N or more between two years (births, deaths and migration included — it is what the UN model projects). “People reaching that age” adds up, for each year after the start, the people who are exactly N that year — the gross number of new potential drivers, before the older ones leave the pool. In the potential-drivers card the pool is people aged N to S−1 (S = the stop-driving age), so net gain = new drivers − drivers lost: new drivers are the people turning N, those who reach S leave the pool for old age, and the rest of the loss (deaths plus net migration among people aged N to S−1) is what is left over once the other two are known — the UN data has no separate death count by age, so it is not a death statistic on its own, and where more people arrive than die it turns into a gain. Ages 60 to 100+ come from a separate single-year file, loaded the first time the stop-driving slider leaves its default.
  5. Regions are the UN's six continental regions (Africa, Asia, Europe, Latin America and the Caribbean, Northern America, Oceania) and 22 subregions (the UN's 21 plus Northern America, which has no subregions of its own). Every country and region figure is the UN's own series; the totals add up (regions equal the sum of their countries to within rounding). Country names follow the rest of this site (e.g. United States, Turkey, South Korea).
  6. % growth rankings ignore very small areas (default: under 1 million people at the start) because a tiny population can post a huge percentage on a few thousand people.
  7. Stored in the database: table world_population_by_age — one row per area and year, one column per exact age (age_0 … age_100, the last meaning 100 and over), 1950–2100, with a flag for projected years; view world_population_age_groups gives under 16 / 16–64 / 65+ / 18+ / 80+. Rebuild with scripts/world_population/build_world_population.py and load_to_supabase.py.
  8. Cross-check with the World Bank: for the 102 countries that this site's Population & Energy page also covers, the World Bank's 2024 population differs from the UN's 1 July 2024 figure by a median of 0.0% (84 countries within 2%, 96 within 5%, world total 6.62 billion against 6.64 billion for the same countries). The largest gaps are Moldova and Albania, where national statistics offices publish far lower resident counts than the UN does.
  9. Not (yet) included: the US Census Bureau International Database (an independent series worth comparing later), the UN low and high projection variants, and the split by sex.
