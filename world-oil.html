How these numbers are built — read before quoting them
  1. Total oil consumed, 1950–2025: Energy Institute Statistical Review (via Our World in Data), converted from TWh at 579 TWh per million barrels a day. It is the volume of oil burned or used as raw material, including shipping and aviation fuel. The International Energy Agency (IEA) counts about 8% more (103 mb/d in 2024) because it adds biofuels and all natural-gas liquids. 2026 = 2025 grown by the IEA's 2026 forecast (+0.7%).
  2. Purposes 1971–2019 are IEA data. The IEA's Key World Energy Statistics 2021 publishes a chart of oil final consumption by sector (road, aviation, navigation, rail, industry, homes, non-energy use, other). We read the values straight from the chart's vector drawing; they reproduce the printed 1973 and 2019 shares to the decimal (for instance road 30.8% then 49.2%, 94 then 169 EJ). Energy is turned into barrels at 2.1 EJ per mb/d.
  3. Cars vs trucks is an estimate. The IEA does not split road oil. We anchor it on the IEA 2017 truck study (trucks use about 17 mb/d, roughly 44% of road oil) and assume trucks, vans and buses were 25% of road oil in 1971, rising evenly to 44% in 2017. Cars, SUVs and motorbikes are the rest. IEA and other estimates put cars at 21–24 mb/d, the same range as our 22.
  4. 1950–1970 are estimates: the IEA series starts in 1971, so we keep the 1971 shares and the 1971 ratio between final consumption and total oil. In reality transport was probably smaller and fuel oil for heat and industry larger. 2020–2026 are estimates too: each sector follows the IEA Oil 2025 forecast of the fuel it burns (gasoline for cars, diesel for trucks, jet fuel for planes, fuel oil for ships, LPG and naphtha for chemicals). Hatched bands mark these years in the first chart.
  5. “Power plants, refineries & other conversion” is what remains of total oil after the IEA's final-consumption sectors: oil burnt for electricity, refinery fuel, losses and the definition gap between the two sources.
  6. Production by country, 1960–2025: Energy Institute / Our World in Data, crude oil and condensate. Russia includes the Soviet Union before 1985 (the data only splits the republics from 1985). The United States figures of chart 3 come from the US Energy Information Administration (Monthly Energy Review).
