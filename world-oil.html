4 · What if…? Build your own oil scenario

Move the levers and watch world oil demand change from today to 2060. Dashed line = current trends (every lever left at its default); dotted flat line = the starting level, to see whether demand ends above or below it. The scenarios start from the pre-war 2026 level (97.1 mb/d), not from the lower 2026 of section 1 (the Strait of Hormuz crisis, forecast by the IEA at 94.0 mb/d on this page's basis), because a crisis year is not a base for a 2060 path. The chart is a simple fleet model, not a forecast: read the assumptions below. “Electric” means battery-electric; plug-in hybrids still burn fuel.

    Model assumptions (editable above where marked)
    1. Starting point 2026 = the purposes of chart 1 (cars 22 mb/d, trucks, vans and buses 17, aviation 7, shipping 6, plastics and chemicals 16…). The 2026 world car fleet (1.35 billion) and its 4.9% battery-electric share come from the World Car Sales page.
    2. Electric cars and trucks: each year 5% of the car fleet (6% of the truck fleet) is scrapped and the fleet grows 1% (1.5%). New vehicles follow the electric sales share you choose, rising evenly from today's level (cars 18.8% in 2026, trucks about 10%) to your target at the target year, then held. Oil use of a fleet = what the same fleet would burn without electrics × the share of the fleet that is not electric. Electric vehicles are assumed to be driven like the vehicles they replace.
    3. Current trends (the defaults): electric new-car share 35% in 2030, 60% in 2040, 75% in 2050; electric new-truck share 15%, 30%, 45%; petrol and diesel vehicles gain 1.0% (cars) and 0.8% (trucks) fuel economy a year; air traffic grows 3% a year with 1.2% yearly efficiency gains (the IEA Oil 2025 outlook has jet fuel at +2.1% a year to 2030); shipping traffic +2% a year with 1% efficiency; feedstocks +1.8% a year (IEA: +2.0% LPG/ethane, +2.5% naphtha); industry and power plants, homes (heating and cooking), and farms, services and other oil use each −0.8% a year. These are our assumptions, not IEA scenarios.
    4. Not modelled: price effects, rebound, biofuels in road fuels, ethane (not oil in some statistics), the oil used to make electricity, country differences. Power plants and refinery fuel move in step with final use; the industry and power plants lever also moves them directly, on top of that link, because the data do not say how much of that block is electricity and how much is refinery fuel.

    How these numbers are built — read before quoting them
    1. Total oil consumed, 1950–2025: Energy Institute Statistical Review (via Our World in Data), converted from TWh at 579 TWh per million barrels a day. It is the volume of oil burned or used as raw material, including shipping and aviation fuel. The International Energy Agency (IEA) counts about 8% more (103 mb/d in 2024) because it adds biofuels and all natural-gas liquids. 2026 = 2025 grown by the IEA's 2026 forecast (+0.7%).
    2. Who burns it, by country (sections 8 and 9). 1965–2025 are the Energy Institute's country figures (same conversion as above). 1950–1964 are our estimate, because no free source publishes oil consumption by country before 1965 (the UN's own database, which starts in 1950, is only sold or released on request): each country is carried back from its 1965–69 level using the oil CO₂ series of the Global Carbon Project (via Our World in Data), which rests on the UN energy statistics. Two checks: the countries added up come within 1% (1950), 1% (1955) and 3% (1960) of the world total, and the United States comes out at 6.8, 8.7 and 9.7 million barrels a day in 1950, 1955 and 1960 against 6.5, 8.5 and 9.8 in the US government's own series. Seven oil exporters whose UN-based series jumps more than 2.5 times between two years are left out of 1950–64. Per person uses UN World Population Prospects 2024; before 1985 the USSR is the 15 republics added up.
    3. Purposes 1971–2019 are IEA data. The IEA's Key World Energy Statistics 2021 publishes a chart of oil final consumption by sector (road, aviation, navigation, rail, industry, homes, non-energy use, other). We read the values straight from the chart's vector drawing; they reproduce the printed 1973 and 2019 shares to the decimal (for instance road 30.8% then 49.2%, 94 then 169 EJ). Energy is turned into barrels at 2.1 EJ per mb/d.
    4. Cars vs trucks is an estimate. The IEA does not split road oil. We anchor it on the IEA 2017 truck study (trucks use about 17 mb/d, roughly 44% of road oil) and assume trucks, vans and buses were 25% of road oil in 1971, rising evenly to 44% in 2017. Cars, SUVs and motorbikes are the rest. IEA and other estimates put cars at 21–24 mb/d, the same range as our 22.
    5. 1950–1970 are estimates: the IEA series starts in 1971, so we keep the 1971 shares and the 1971 ratio between final consumption and total oil. In reality transport was probably smaller and fuel oil for heat and industry larger. 2020–2025: the total is real, the split by purpose is estimated. The Energy Institute total for these years is final data, but the IEA sector chart we read ends in 2019, so each sector follows the IEA Oil 2025 demand figures of the fuel it burns (gasoline for cars, diesel for trucks, jet fuel for planes, fuel oil for ships, LPG and naphtha for chemicals): actual demand up to 2024, the IEA's preliminary figure for 2025. 2026 is the IEA's forecast, converted: the IEA Oil Market Report of 14 October 2026 puts 2026 demand at 102.4 mb/d, 2.6 below 2025 (105.0 on its basis); we apply that ratio (0.975) to the Energy Institute's 2025 total (96.4 mb/d), giving 94.0 mb/d, and share the cut across purposes in proportion. The IEA says diesel, petrochemical feedstocks and jet fuel carried most of it, so the split of 2026 is a rough guide. Hatched bands mark the estimated years in the first chart.
    6. “Power plants, refineries & other conversion” is what remains of total oil after the IEA's final-consumption sectors: oil burnt for electricity, refinery fuel, losses and the definition gap between the two sources.
    7. Bridging 2026 until the Energy Institute publishes it (June 2027). The Energy Institute has one release a year. For 2026 we use the IEA Oil Market Reports and convert them with the ratio between the two sources in 2025, the last year both exist: world demand (section 1) from the October 2026 report; oil produced by country (sections 6 and 7) as the January–June 2026 average of the 1Q26 figure of the May report and the 2Q26 figure of the July report (the later reports are not yet public as full tables), each country times its own Energy Institute / IEA ratio of 2025 (the IEA counts crude, condensate and NGL together, the Energy Institute series differs by country, so a country's ratio can be well above or below 1). Countries the IEA does not list are kept at their 2025 level. World production uses the IEA supply without biofuels and refinery gains. Script scripts/world_oil/bridge_2026.py; replace it when the Energy Institute 2026 data arrives.
    8. What-if scenarios (section 4) run a simple fleet model from the pre-war 2026 level; the assumptions are listed under the chart. They illustrate orders of magnitude, they are not forecasts.
    9. Production by country, 1960–2025: Energy Institute / Our World in Data, crude oil and condensate. Russia includes the Soviet Union before 1985 (the data only splits the republics from 1985). The United States figures of chart 3 come from the US Energy Information Administration (Monthly Energy Review).
